# Polynomial Conventions
Polynomials, circuits, and sequences have natural correspondences, but several binary convention choices — coefficient ordering, shift direction, transform domain — are rarely stated explicitly, and different choices swap which polynomial "naturally" corresponds to a given sequence or circuit. This document defines the primal and dual relationships between polynomials and sequences over GF(2), traces how convention choices swap them, and specifies which convention each register type in the library adopts.

## The Primal/Dual Framework

When we say a sequence "satisfies" a polynomial, there are two natural interpretations — one algebraic (roots), one computational (convolution) — reflecting two modes of thinking about feedback registers. These interpretations are not equivalent: they correspond to different polynomials, related by reversal. The library's coefficient convention `[a_0, a_1, ..., a_n]` (see [Notation and Terminology](../conventions/Notation%20and%20Terminology.md)) pins the indexing that makes both interpretations precise and distinguishes the two.
Let $S$ be an eventually periodic sequence over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $P$ a polynomial in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$. Let $\tilde{S}$ denote the sequence with the same periodic orbit in the reversed order, and $\tilde{P}$ the polynomial with reversed coefficient order (the reciprocal polynomial).

**The root perspective.** In the field $\mathbb{F}_{2^n} \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/P$, the polynomial $P$ defines the algebraic structure, and any sequence generated by iterating in this field has its roots among the roots of $P$. We say $P$ satisfies the **primal** relationship with $S$ when every root of $S$ is a root of $P$: that is, $P(\alpha) = 0$ for every root $\alpha$ of $S$. Written $P = \mu(S)$.

This is the Galois way of thinking — the polynomial is the field modulus, and the sequence lives inside it. It is arguably the more fundamental definition: saying that the sequence's roots satisfy the polynomial is the direct algebraic relationship, and it connects immediately to the field structure that Galois LFSR state identification relies on. In a representation defined by $P$ with root $\alpha$, both $x$ and $\alpha$ satisfy $P$, so $P$ satisfies the primal relationship with any sequence generated in this field. The roots of the reciprocal $\tilde{P}$ are the inverse coset $\alpha^{-2^i}$.

**The recurrence perspective.** A sequence satisfies a linear recurrence when some polynomial convolves it to zero. We say $P$ satisfies the **dual** relationship with $S$ when $P$ annihilates $S$ under convolution: $(P * S)[n] = 0$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Written $P = \tilde{\mu}(S)$.

This is the Fibonacci way of thinking — the polynomial describes the recurrence rule that the sequence obeys. It is the perspective that sequence analysis naturally works in: Berlekamp-Massey builds a polynomial until it convolves the input to zero, and generating function denominators encode the recurrence.

**The connection.** These two perspectives are related by polynomial reversal. If $P$ satisfies the primal relationship with $S$ (roots of $S$ are roots of $P$), then $\tilde{P}$ satisfies the dual relationship with the same $S$ ($\tilde{P} * S = 0$) — and vice versa. Equivalently: the primal condition can also be stated as $\tilde{P} * S = 0$, while the dual condition states $P * S = 0$. The primal polynomial $\mu(S)$ and dual polynomial $\tilde{\mu}(S)$ of a given sequence are always reverses of each other.

The literature adopts one perspective or the other as "the" minimal polynomial. Neither alone provides a framework for moving between the two — the primal/dual distinction does.

**Existence and uniqueness.** Both $\mu(S)$ and $\tilde{\mu}(S)$ exist and are unique for every eventually periodic $S$. The set $I = \{P \in \mathbb{F}_2[x] : P * S = 0 \text{ eventually}\}$ is an ideal of $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, nontrivial because $1 + x^T \in I$ whenever $S$ has period $T$. Since $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ is a PID, $I$ has a unique monic generator of minimal degree — this is $\tilde{\mu}(S)$. The primal $\mu(S) = \widetilde{\tilde{\mu}(S)}$ is its reverse; uniqueness follows because reversal is a bijection on monic polynomials of each degree.

**Terminology.** Primal and dual name **relationships** between a polynomial and a sequence, not inherent properties of either. A given polynomial can satisfy the primal relationship with one sequence and the dual relationship with another. When the pairing is unambiguous, "the primal polynomial" and "the dual polynomial" are shorthand for "the polynomial satisfying the primal/dual relationship with this circuit's output." Similarly, for a fixed polynomial $P$, "the primal circuit" means the circuit whose output satisfies the primal relationship with $P$, and "the dual circuit" the one satisfying the dual relationship.

### Symmetry Relations

Let $S$ be a sequence, $P$ a polynomial, and $\tilde{S}$, $\tilde{P}$ their reversals. The primal/dual relationship is governed by two independent facts:

1. **Polynomial reversal swaps the relationship** (sequence fixed): $P$ satisfies the primal relationship with $S$ iff $\tilde{P}$ satisfies the dual relationship with $S$. Immediate from the definitions — both conditions state $\tilde{P} * S = 0$.

2. **Simultaneous reversal preserves the relationship**: $P$ satisfies the primal relationship with $S$ iff $\tilde{P}$ satisfies the primal relationship with $\tilde{S}$. Reversing a polynomial inverts its roots ($\alpha \mapsto \alpha^{-1}$); reversing a sequence inverts its roots likewise. Root containment is preserved under simultaneous inversion.

Together these give the four-way equivalence:

$$P \text{ primal with } S \iff \tilde{P} \text{ dual with } S \iff \tilde{P} \text{ primal with } \tilde{S} \iff P \text{ dual with } \tilde{S}$$

As a corollary, reversing the sequence alone also swaps the relationship (combine both facts), and the set of primitive polynomials is closed under reversal.

## Convention Choices and Their Effects

The definitions above are abstract: given a register, its constructor polynomial satisfies one particular relationship (primal or dual) with the output sequence, determined by the implementation's convention choices. But in practice, these conventions are rarely stated explicitly. The choice of generating function domain, shift direction, and register architecture each introduce an implicit reversal that swaps which relationship the "natural" polynomial satisfies. The rest of this document traces these swaps and specifies how each register type in the library resolves them.


## Generating Function Conventions

The choice of generating function exponent convention swaps the primal and dual roles, in the same way that reversing the coefficient array does.

### D-Transform (Forward Exponents)

Literature from mathematics, combinatorics, and recurrence sequences uses generating functions with forward-progressing exponents: $\{1, D, D^2, D^3, \ldots\}$. Under this convention, multiplying by a polynomial is equivalent to convolution. The time-reversal implicit in convolution (high-degree polynomial coefficients select low-index sequence terms) means the **dual** polynomial convolves with the sequence to produce the zero sequence, while the primal does not.

### Z-Transform (Inverse Exponents)

Engineering and control-theory literature uses $\{1, z^{-1}, z^{-2}, z^{-3}, \ldots\}$, which effectively reverses the sequence indexing. Under this convention, the **primal** polynomial convolves to zero, while the dual does not.

The library adopts the D-transform convention, both because it matches the coefficient indexing convention and to align with the theory-oriented literature.

## Hardware Conventions

The primal relationship connects directly to the exponential representation, and thus to Galois LFSR state identification where the register state is an element of $\mathbb{F}_{2^n} \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/P$.

### Shift Direction

When the register state is identified with $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/P$:

- A **shift up** (values progress toward higher indices) is multiplication by $x$.
- A **shift down** (values progress toward lower indices) is multiplication by $x^{-1}$.

As the register iterates, shift-up produces a sequence with increasing exponents while shift-down produces decreasing exponents, which reverses the sequence indexing and swaps the polynomial's primal/dual role — exactly as the D/Z-transform choice does.

Authors historically reconcile this with a variety of unspoken "flips", including implicitly using the reciprocal polynomial $\tilde{P}$ as the modulus, reversing the sequence indexing, changing the minimal polynomial definition, or flipping the MSB/LSB labeling (effectively converting a shift-down register to shift-up). These implicit choices are a persistent source of confusion in the literature.


## The Ambiguity in Practice

To make this concrete: the statement "the 3-bit Fibonacci LFSR with polynomial $x^3 + x + 1$" could refer to any of the following four update matrices, depending on shift direction and whether the polynomial satisfies the primal or dual relationship with the output:

$$
\begin{align*}
& \text{Shift Up Primal} &
& \text{Shift Down Primal} \\
&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
&&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\\
& \text{Shift Up Dual} &
& \text{Shift Down Dual} \\
&
\begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
&&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
$$

The same ambiguity applies to "the 3-bit Galois LFSR with polynomial $x^3 + x + 1$":

$$
\begin{align*}
& \text{Shift Up Primal} &
& \text{Shift Down Primal} \\
&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 1 \\
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
&&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    1 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\\
& \text{Shift Up Dual} &
& \text{Shift Down Dual} \\
&
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix}
&&
\begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
$$

Each of the eight matrices above is a valid interpretation. The convention sections below identify which axes (transform domain, shift direction) produce these variants, and the Register Conventions section specifies the library's particular choices: Fibonacci and Galois both use **shift-down dual** (bottom-right in each grid); MPR uses **shift-up primal** (top-left in the Galois grid).

## Conventions for PyPR

The conventions below are the particular choices this library makes — other implementations may resolve the primal/dual ambiguity differently. The framework above makes each choice explicit rather than leaving it implicit. This section also aims to explain the relevant considerations and why we chose the conventions we did, and the consequences.

### Berlekamp-Massey Output

The Berlekamp-Massey algorithm builds a polynomial by iteratively correcting it until it convolves with the input sequence to produce zero. Its output therefore naturally satisfies the **dual** relationship with the input sequence: `convolve(BKM_output, seq) = 0`.

The library preserves this dual output rather than reversing it, for two reasons: it matches the historical LFSR literature's convention, and it makes the register constructors round-trip cleanly — since both Fibonacci and Galois also expect the dual polynomial as input, `Fibonacci(*Fibonacci.fromSeq(seq))` and `Galois(*Galois.fromSeq(seq))` reproduce `seq` with no manual reversal.

### Fibonacci LFSR

The constructor input $P$ satisfies the **dual** relationship with the output: $P = \tilde{\mu}(S)$, so $P * S = 0$.

In our view, it feels more natural for Fibonacci registers shift down (and indeed this matches the historical literature). To support this, Internally, $P$ is reversed to build taps at positions aligned with $\tilde{P}$ (the primal polynomial for this circuit), producing a shift-down register that generates the sequence satisfying $P$ dually. This matches the most common historical convention.

### Galois LFSR

The constructor input $P$ satisfies the **dual** relationship with the output: $P = \tilde{\mu}(S)$, so $P * S = 0$.

The register state is identified with $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/P$ and stored as the column vector $(b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{n-1})^\mathsf{T}$, with coordinate 0 at the top as specified by [Matrix Indexing](Matrix%20Indexing.md). In our view, shift-up is more natural for Galois registers (which is why MPR uses it), but for compatibility with Fibonacci (same constructor polynomial yields same output sequence), and to match the most common pattern in the literature, we chose to also use a shift-down implementation for our Galois LFSRs. The downward shift means the iteration is actually multiplication by $x^{-1}$. Rather than reversing the polynomial to build taps, the implementation uses the dual polynomial shifted one index toward zero. This matches most circuit diagrams in the literature, although most authors resolve the implicit $x^{-1}$ multiplication via relabeling rather than stating the connection explicitly.

In the literature, the Galois taps are described as "aligned with the polynomial" — the nonzero coefficients directly give the tap positions. This is true here: the taps are aligned with the constructor input $P$ (which satisfies the dual relationship with the output). Precisely, bit $i$ receives the departing bit's value iff $P_{i+1} \neq 0$.

### MPR (Mersenne Product Register)

The constructor input $P$ satisfies the **primal** relationship with the output: $P = \mu(S)$, so $\tilde{P} * S = 0$.

The register state is identified with $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/P$ and shifts upward by default ($U(x) = x$), so there is no implicit reversal.

### MPR-Galois LFSR equivalence
Note that reversing the update polynomial on an MPR changes the shift direction, reproducing the hardware for a Galois LFSR exactly

```python
# Circuit equivalent to a downward shifting Galois (multiplication by x^{-1})
MPR(n, P, update_poly=P[1:])

# The MPR update is the inverse transition, (multiplication by x)
MPR(n, P)
```

Here `P[1:]` drops the constant coefficient and shifts the remaining
coefficients down by one index. For a primitive polynomial, $P(0)=1$, so if
$P(x)=1+xQ(x)$ then $xQ(x)=P(x)+1\equiv1\pmod{P(x)}$. Thus `P[1:]` represents
$x^{-1}$ modulo $P$

### Round-Trip Behavior

The intended behavior of this convention set is:

- Galois and Fibonacci generate the same sequence from bit 0 when constructed with the same polynomial.
- `Fibonacci(*Fibonacci.fromSeq(seq))` and `Galois(*Galois.fromSeq(seq))` reproduce `seq`.
- `convolve(BKM_output, seq)` yields a finite sequence.

The MPR identities above are exact transition-map identities. The current
Fibonacci implementation still has an indexing defect for the ordinary
degree-3 example, so its runtime equivalence requires that constructor issue
to be fixed separately.
